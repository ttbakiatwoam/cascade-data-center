## Evidence rules

- **Verified public record / peer-reviewed literature:** original official minutes, parcel data, standards, and papers.
- **Company or cooperative statement:** evidence of what a representative said, not a signed service agreement or completed engineering report.
- **Draft proposal:** research project recommendations, **not current City zoning law**.
- **Unresolved:** specific equipment, sound model, permits, measurements, or contracts not yet public.
- **Cross-industry comparison:** useful context only when differences in size, equipment, climate, and operations are disclosed.

## Objection 1: Will a persistent low-frequency hum or ground vibration create medical problems?

**What research shows:** dBC retains more low-frequency sound energy than dBA. It is **not** a frequency, nor a direct measurement of building vibration. The 2016 systematic review of seven observational studies reported associations between low-frequency noise and annoyance, sleep problems, concentration difficulty, and headache, but evidence for chronic medical disorders was especially limited. A 2023 double-blind laboratory study exposed noise-sensitive, otherwise healthy adults to 72 hours of **inaudible simulated wind-turbine infrasound** and observed no worsening of measured outcomes compared with sham, while audible traffic noise disrupted sleep. Neither study proves that every audible equipment hum is harmless. Nor is there support for the categorical claim that only people with prior conditions can be affected by sound.

**What Cascade has:** On October 1, the company referred to preliminary cooling/property-line levels around 50 dB and discussed a possible 65 dB property-line upper limit. The company cautioned that hot-weather cooling is not guaranteed to stay at 50 dB. There is no final project-specific dBA/dBC/tonal/full-load/nighttime residential model in the file.

**Suitable evidence/controls:** record background, source spectrum, nighttime dBA and dBC, prominent tones, hot-weather operation, generator tests, building vibration if relevant, receptor modeling, independent post-construction verification, complaint-triggered testing.

## Objection 2: Does industrial noise drive away predators and bring nesting birds back?

**Documented ecological finding:** A 2009 peer-reviewed study of noisy natural-gas compressor sites in New Mexico found reduced nesting bird-species richness but higher reproductive success for some birds nesting in noisy habitat due to changes in predator-prey interactions, including nest predation. The sites were **not data centers**. This study does **not** demonstrate that cats, foxes, or other mammalian predators leave data centers, that insects increase, or that the Cascade facility will restore wildlife habitat. Species-specific responses can be positive and negative simultaneously.

Source: [Francis, Ortega, Cruz (2009), Current Biology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19631542/). See [expanded noise research](../impacts/08_NOISE_AND_ACOUSTICS.md).

## Objection 3: Are 100-200 ft setbacks measured from front doors?

**No.** The [project-generated draft ordinance](../../proposals/DRAFT-ORDINANCE-M2-DATA-CENTER-PERFORMANCE-STANDARDS-2026-10-06.md) proposes 100 ft from an adjoining residential zoning boundary for major industrial buildings and 200 ft for large external mechanical equipment, with a narrowly defined independent-study reduction option for equipment. These are **proposed** zoning-boundary setbacks, **not adopted standards or verified building-to-home distances**. The October 1 preliminary plan described building setbacks around **216-291 ft** on portions of the site, but final geometries and distances to each dwelling remain unresolved. Under ideal point-source free-field conditions sound declines around 6 dB per doubling of distance; actual multiple industrial sources, reflections, terrain, barriers, and weather require modeling.

Sources: [October 1 presentation record](../updates/28_SIMPLE_MINING_COMMUNITY_FORUM_2026-10-01.md), [noise analysis](../impacts/08_NOISE_AND_ACOUSTICS.md), [parcel/zoning records](../project/29_SIMPLE_MINING_SITE_PARCEL_AND_ZONING.md).

## Objection 4: What about approximately 15 diesel generators and emissions?

The October 1 company presentation estimated **approximately 15 diesel standby units** rather than continuous on-site primary generation. Exact engines, fuel storage, test schedules, emissions, permit coverage, runtime, and acoustic limits are **unknown**. State air permitting and approved generator records should establish enforceable operational facts. Do not state that all generators run only rarely until the approved testing/emergency operating schedule is known.

The August 12, 2026 Cascade Municipal Utilities Board minutes list a **$14,375.18** payment to McDermott Oil Co. under August bills/claims. This is a documented vendor payment, **not independently itemized proof of fuel volumes, use solely by generators, July runtime, or 15 days of operation**. ## Objection 5: Will the data center consume electricity reserved for homes or raise rates?

On October 1, **Maquoketa Valley Electric Cooperative** described a capacity study performed with **Central Iowa Power Cooperative** finding transmission capacity for the proposed approximately 40 MW load while reserving capacity for ordinary/native growth. It said a large load would need additional generation and **Simple Mining would pay 100% of project-driven transmission/substation/distribution upgrades upfront**, subject to true-up. The representative expected a **neutral or positive** effect on cooperative rates if structured as described. He distinguished rate increases for other causes, including a previous April increase and potential future increases, from data-center cost-shifting. He said added fixed-cost recovery could support rate stability. **This is not a guarantee of flat bills**, and a Cascade service agreement had not been executed by October 1.

## Objection 6: Do lithium-ion batteries present an uncontrollable fire risk?

Thermal runaway can propagate and release flammable/toxic gases, sometimes causing fire, deflagration, or reignition. This is a **real engineering and emergency response hazard**, not proof that the proposed building contains lithium-ion batteries. Fire services address it with site-specific pre-incident plans, trained teams, PPE, atmospheric monitoring, appropriate cooling/containment and exposure protection, and follow-up monitoring. A clean-agent release alone is **not assured to arrest thermal runaway**.

The 2019 Arizona 2.16 MWh BESS deflagration seriously injured four firefighters; its design and scale must not be attributed to Cascade.

## Objection 7: Are lead-acid batteries always safer and cheaper over time?

VRLA lead-acid is a long-standing UPS technology, usually **lower cost upfront**, but lithium-ion is being adopted for UPS uses and may have **lower lifetime cost** through longer replacement intervals and lower maintenance. Both chemistries have distinct fire, toxic-material, and electrical risks. Installed chemistry, life-cycle cost, and UL/code controls **cannot be inferred for Cascade**.

A smartphone and a large data-center UPS might both employ lithium-ion cells; their **per-unit fire probabilities are not established as equal**. Comparing one handheld device to an industrial battery installation without exposure denominators, pack design, and stored energy is unsound.

## Objection 8: Which fire-suppression equipment is required, and who is responsible?

Data centers may combine smoke/early detection, automatic sprinkler or pre-action sprinklers, clean-agent suppression, monitored power shutdowns, rated compartments, and battery-specific protection. Details are governed by adopted code and approved engineering. NFPA 75, NFPA 855, NFPA 13, and UL 9540/9540A may be relevant based on application. **There is no confirmed Cascade design.**

Central UPS design is often facility-controlled. A tenant **may** be permitted to supply rack UPS according to lease and facility rules, but the October 1 forum did **not** establish customer-owned racks/batteries or control allocation. Regardless of private ownership, building/fire/electrical code requirements and operator approvals still apply. Contractual delegation does not void them.

## Objection 9: Will cooling deplete city water or leak coolant?

The company represented that its current Cascade concept uses **closed-loop liquid cooling with dry/air-cooled outdoor heat rejection**, not routine evaporation from city water. Domestic use and possible humidification were mentioned. This is preliminary, not a final water budget. A sealed cooling design, gallons/day and gallons/year, coolant inventory/SDS, containment, sewer and stormwater planning, and a legally enforceable cap would test the claim.

## Objection 11: Can zoning forbid private customers, AI software, or cryptocurrencies?

Iowa Code Chapter 414 gives cities zoning authority over land use, structures, and physically measurable impacts. The City **does not possess a general zoning power to approve lawful computer users, online content, or private software**, but private property is **not exempt** from zoning, building, fire, electric, or environmental obligations. Any proposed tenant- or workload-specific restriction needs legal review; neutral physical-impact standards are a more direct zoning approach. Workload switches can matter if they materially change the approved noise, MW, water, cooling, or emergency risk profile.

## Objection 12: Will property values decline?

A change in nearby property value is **not an established effect for this specific proposal**. Research on distant hyperscale sites cannot be presented as a causal estimate for a 60,000-square-foot building on a 15.22-acre industrial parcel. Document study facility size, residential proximity, comparison groups, market controls, construction/operations period, and publication design before importing an estimate. Property-specific appraisal or paired-sales work would be a stronger local test.

## Objection 13: How will future expansions and closure be handled?

The 40 MW, generators, and other concept values are **not yet final permit caps**. The research recommends defining the permitted operating envelope, renewed review for major expansion, annual compliance, hazard inventory updates, and a funded decommissioning plan. These are **proposals**, not current enforceable conditions.

## Priority items to verify before relying on project-specific assurances

1. Executed power-generation, service, and cost-allocation agreements; public capacity-study summary.
2. Final stamped site and equipment plans, surveyed home/receptor distances, and residential sound model including dBA, dBC, tones, and hot-weather full-load conditions.
3. Generator specifications, emissions permits, sound levels, fuel storage, and operating/testing logs.
4. Battery ownership, UPS/BESS distinction, chemistry, capacity, safety certification, suppression and evacuation plans, local fire-service review.
5. Cooling engineering, water/drought cap, spill response, and final stormwater approval.
6. Monitoring, enforcement, reporting, infrastructure costs, and closure security.
