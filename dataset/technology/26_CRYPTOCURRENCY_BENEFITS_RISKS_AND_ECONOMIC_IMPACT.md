# Cryptocurrency: Benefits, Risks, and Economic Impact

> Research context for the Cascade Data Center project. This file documents benefits that cryptocurrencies and crypto-based payment systems have **actually demonstrated**, while also preserving evidence of illicit-finance, consumer, volatility, and energy risks. It does not assume that every blockchain application is a cryptocurrency benefit or that the proposed Cascade facility will produce any particular economic outcome.
>
> Last reviewed: 2026-09-10

## Research principle

Cryptocurrency is unusually easy to discuss in absolutes. It is neither accurate to describe it only as speculative gambling/criminal infrastructure nor to describe it as a universally democratizing replacement for banking.

The evidence supports a more useful conclusion: crypto created a globally accessible digital-asset and settlement ecosystem worth trillions of dollars at various market peaks, introduced new forms of peer-to-peer ownership and programmable finance, enabled some lower-friction cross-border and humanitarian transfers, and became integrated into mainstream regulated investment markets. It has also enabled or facilitated significant fraud, theft, ransomware, sanctions evasion, money laundering, and darknet commerce and can impose substantial electricity demand where proof-of-work mining is used.

Those facts can coexist.

---

# 1. What Bitcoin originally changed

The 2008 Bitcoin white paper proposed a peer-to-peer electronic cash system in which online payments could be sent directly between parties without relying on a financial institution as the transaction intermediary. Its core innovations combined public-key cryptography, a distributed timestamped ledger, proof-of-work consensus, and an issuance rule enforced by network software.

**Original source:**
- Satoshi Nakamoto, “Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System”  
  https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf

Bitcoin did not invent cryptography, digital signatures, peer-to-peer networking, or proof-of-work individually. Its historical importance was combining prior ideas into an open network that maintained scarce digital units without a central issuing bank or company.

# 2. Open participation and self-custody

A basic form of “democratization” created by cryptocurrency is architectural: a person does not need permission from a bank, brokerage, card network, or national clearinghouse simply to generate a cryptographic key and receive a native blockchain asset.

That does **not** mean access is equally useful to everyone. Users still need connectivity, technical literacy, a way to acquire assets, and protection from scams, lost keys, volatility, and coercion. Converting crypto to local currency may still require regulated intermediaries and identification.

The strongest evidence for financial democratization is therefore not the ideology of permissionlessness alone, but real-world data showing where people actually use digital assets for payments and transfers.

# 3. U.S. household use: investment dominates, but transactional use reaches some underserved users

The Federal Reserve’s 2025 Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking found:

- **10%** of U.S. adults used cryptocurrency for any surveyed purpose in 2025;
- **9%** bought or held cryptocurrency as an investment;
- **2%** used cryptocurrency to buy something or make a payment;
- **1%** used cryptocurrency to send money to friends or family.

Among respondents who used cryptocurrency for financial transactions, the reported main reasons included:

- recipient/business preference — **26%**;
- faster transfer — **19%**;
- privacy — **17%**;
- cheaper transfer — **14%**;
- perceived safety — **9%**;
- distrust of banks — **7%**.

Among the relatively small sample who sent cryptocurrency to friends or family, **33%** said at least one recipient lived outside the United States.

The Federal Reserve also reports that transactional crypto use is higher among some groups with weaker access to mainstream finance, including unbanked adults and users of nonbank check-cashing/money-order services. This does not prove that crypto is solving financial exclusion at population scale, but it is evidence that its payment function is disproportionately relevant to some users outside conventional banking.

**Primary / official sources:**
- Federal Reserve — 2025 SHED, Banking / Cryptocurrency  
  https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-banking.htm
- Federal Reserve — SHED 2025 Supplemental Appendix B

# 4. Cross-border payments and remittances

## Nigeria: a documented large-scale stablecoin use case

The International Monetary Fund reported in June 2026 that stablecoins had become a meaningful cross-border payments channel in Nigeria. The IMF describes households and small firms using smartphones, digital wallets, and U.S.-dollar-pegged crypto assets to receive remittances and make cross-border payments in minutes, often at lower cost than traditional channels.

The IMF reported approximately **$59 billion in crypto-asset inflows to Nigeria between July 2023 and June 2024** and said Nigeria accounted for roughly **60% of stablecoin inflows into sub-Saharan Africa since 2019**. It identified faster and cheaper cross-border payments, trade, remittances, and financial inclusion as potential benefits while also warning about dollarization, monetary-policy, regulatory, and financial-stability risks.

**Primary / official:**
- IMF — “Stablecoins in Nigeria: A Growing Cross-Border Channel” (2026)  
  https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/06/16/stablecoins-in-nigeria
- IMF — Nigeria 2026 Article IV, stablecoin analysis  
  https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2026/125/article-A001-en.xml

## Broader IMF assessment

The IMF has stated that stablecoins may reduce the cost and increase the speed of cross-border payments and remittances, increase competition, support financial inclusion, and enable programmable payment. The same IMF analysis warns about fragmentation, run risk, currency substitution, capital-flow volatility, and regulatory gaps.

- IMF Financial Counsellor — “Tokenized Finance and Money” (2026)  
  https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/05/11/sp051126-tokenized-finance-and-money
- IMF — “How Stablecoins Can Improve Payments and Global Finance” (2025)  
  https://www.imf.org/en/blogs/articles/2025/12/04/how-stablecoins-can-improve-payments-and-global-finance

# 5. Humanitarian payments during war and displacement

In December 2022, UNHCR launched a pilot in Ukraine that distributed humanitarian assistance in **USDC**, a U.S.-dollar stablecoin, directly to eligible recipients’ digital wallets. The design allowed recipients to carry the value digitally while displaced and convert it to dollars, euros, or local currency through MoneyGram locations, including more than 4,500 locations in Ukraine at the time.

UNHCR said the assistance was intended for basic needs including rent, food, medical care, and heating.

This is a concrete cryptocurrency/stablecoin benefit rather than merely a generic “blockchain” example because recipients were actually paid in a crypto asset.

**Primary / official:**
- UNHCR Ukraine — blockchain/stablecoin humanitarian payment pilot  
  https://www.unhcr.org/ua/en/news/unhcr-launches-pilot-cash-based-intervention-using-blockchain-technology-humanitarian-payments
- UNHCR — 2023 follow-up / award  
  https://www.unhcr.org/us/news/press-releases/unhcr-wins-award-innovative-use-blockchain-solutions-provide-cash-forcibly

# 6. A new asset class and measurable market value

Cryptocurrency created an investable asset class that did not exist before Bitcoin. Its aggregate market value has been highly volatile but has reached multi-trillion-dollar scale.

The IMF reported that global cryptocurrency market capitalization reached approximately **$3.9 trillion at the end of December 2024**, up sharply from earlier years. The U.S. Financial Stability Oversight Council reported a total crypto-asset market value of **$2.35 trillion** in its 2024 annual report at its measurement point, compared with a roughly $48 trillion S&P 500 market capitalization.

The Federal Reserve reported in 2026 that stablecoins alone had reached roughly **$317–320 billion** in market capitalization, with annual settlement volumes measured in the trillions.

**Primary / official or institutional sources:**
- IMF — “Regulating the Crypto Market in Nigeria”  
  https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/002/2025/158/article-A002-en.xml
- Federal Reserve — “Stablecoins in 2025: Developments and Financial Stability Implications”  
  https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/stablecoins-in-2025-developments-and-financial-stability-implications-20260408.html
- Federal Reserve — “Banks in the Age of Stablecoins”  
  https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/banks-in-the-age-of-stablecoins-lessons-from-their-historical-responses-to-financial-innovations-20260501.html

## Important accounting caution: market capitalization is not the same as wealth distributed to society

A market capitalization is the current market price multiplied by outstanding units. It measures the market value assigned to an asset class; it is **not** the same as the amount of cash invested, cumulative realized profit, household net wealth created, or wealth distributed equally among users.

Crypto clearly created assets worth trillions of dollars at various points and produced very large gains for some early and successful holders. It also produced large losses for other participants during crashes, frauds, bankruptcies, hacks, and failed projects. A credible research repository should not turn “$3.9 trillion market cap” into “$3.9 trillion of net social wealth created.”

# 7. Integration into mainstream financial markets

On January 10, 2024, the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission approved rule changes permitting multiple spot Bitcoin exchange-traded products to list and trade on U.S. national securities exchanges. This gave brokerage and retirement-account investors a regulated securities-market vehicle for Bitcoin exposure without personally managing private keys or buying on a crypto exchange.

The SEC emphasized that the approval did **not** constitute an endorsement of Bitcoin and explicitly warned investors about volatility and illicit-use risks.

Federal Reserve researchers later reported that Bitcoin and Ether crypto ETPs had attracted significant inflows; by late December 2024 the group represented roughly **$100 billion in aggregate market capitalization**, and available institutional-filings data suggested a large share of outstanding ETP shares was owned by retail and other smaller investors.

**Primary / official:**
- SEC Chair statement on spot Bitcoin ETP approval  
  https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/speeches-statements/gensler-statement-spot-bitcoin-011023
- Federal Reserve — “Crypto ETPs: An Examination of Liquidity and NAV Premium”  
  https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/crypto-etps-an-examination-of-liquidity-and-nav-premium-20250328.html

# 8. Programmable finance and decentralized applications

Ethereum generalized the idea of a blockchain from recording ownership/transfers of a native currency to executing programs (“smart contracts”) whose state is replicated across the network. That architecture enabled decentralized exchanges, collateralized lending, automated market makers, tokenized assets, non-fungible tokens, decentralized autonomous organizations, and many other experiments.

**Original source / technical history:**
- Ethereum white paper  
  https://ethereum.org/en/whitepaper/

This innovation has produced real financial infrastructure and experimentation, but “decentralized finance” should not automatically be equated with safe finance. Smart-contract exploits, governance failures, leverage, scams, and inadequate anti-money-laundering controls are documented risks.

# 9. Dollar access and stores of transaction value in developing economies

Federal Reserve research notes that dollar-denominated stablecoins are increasingly used as alternatives to physical U.S. dollar banknotes in some developing countries. The IMF similarly documents demand for stablecoins in countries facing inflation, exchange-rate volatility, foreign-exchange restrictions, expensive remittance channels, or limited access to formal banking.

For users in those settings, crypto rails can provide a form of digital access to dollar-denominated value without needing to hold physical banknotes. The trade-off is that widespread foreign-currency stablecoin use can weaken local monetary sovereignty and transmit risks from private stablecoin issuers.

**Primary / official:**
- Federal Reserve — “The International Role of the U.S. Dollar – 2025 Edition”  
  https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/the-international-role-of-the-u-s-dollar-2025-edition-20250718.html
- IMF — “Stablecoins in Nigeria: A Growing Cross-Border Channel”  
  https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2026/06/16/stablecoins-in-nigeria

# 10. Flexible electrical demand from Bitcoin mining

Proof-of-work cryptocurrency mining consumes significant electricity. That is an environmental and grid-planning cost, not a benefit by itself.

However, Bitcoin mining also has an operational characteristic that can be useful under some grid designs: mining loads can shut down rapidly when electricity becomes scarce or expensive. ERCOT created a voluntary curtailment program for large flexible customers, explicitly including Bitcoin mining facilities, to reduce power consumption during high-demand periods. The U.S. Energy Information Administration reports that crypto miners have become regular participants in demand-response programs and can reduce or temporarily shut operations during peak pricing.

ERCOT’s later resource-adequacy analysis also modeled cryptocurrency mining as a price-responsive “Large Flexible Load,” unlike conventional firm loads, because miners can reduce consumption before emergency conditions.

This benefit is **conditional**. A large flexible load can still require major transmission/generation investment and increase total electricity demand. Demand-response value depends on interconnection rules, pricing, actual curtailment performance, and whether the facility bears appropriate infrastructure costs.

**Primary / official:**
- ERCOT — Voluntary Curtailment Program for Large Flexible Customers (2022)  
  https://www.ercot.com/news/release/2022-12-06-ercot-creates-voluntary
- U.S. EIA — “Tracking electricity consumption from U.S. cryptocurrency mining operations” (2024)  
  https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=61364

# 11. Where the “democratization” claim fails or remains unproven

A balanced repository should preserve evidence against its own strongest narrative.

El Salvador made Bitcoin legal tender and spent public money on a national wallet and transaction subsidies. In a 2025 Selected Issues paper, the IMF concluded that Bitcoin adoption **had not produced visible improvements in financial inclusion or digital remittances**, usage by individuals and firms remained minimal, and there was no evidence of a beneficial Bitcoin use case for the unbanked population in that program.

That is an important counterexample: cryptocurrency’s technical openness does not guarantee mass adoption, financial inclusion, lower remittance costs, or good public policy.

**Primary / official:**
- IMF — *El Salvador: Selected Issues* (2025)  
  https://www.imf.org/en/publications/cr/issues/2025/03/19/el-salvador-selected-issues-565394

# 12. Illicit markets, drugs, money laundering, ransomware, and sanctions evasion

Cryptocurrency has been used extensively for illegal commerce and laundering. This should not be omitted from a benefits analysis.

## Darknet drug markets

The U.S. Department of Justice reported that Hydra Market—the world’s largest and longest-running darknet market before its 2022 seizure—facilitated illegal drugs, stolen financial information, fraudulent documents, and money-laundering services. DOJ estimated that Hydra accounted for about **80% of darknet-market-related cryptocurrency transactions in 2021** and had received approximately **$5.2 billion in cryptocurrency since 2015**.

- DOJ — Hydra Market seizure  
  https://www.justice.gov/usao-ndca/pr/justice-department-investigation-leads-shutdown-largest-online-darknet-marketplace

## Money laundering and ransomware

DOJ reported that the Bitzlato exchange processed more than **$700 million** in cryptocurrency connected to Hydra users and also received millions of dollars in ransomware proceeds. Treasury’s DeFi illicit-finance assessment documents use by cybercriminals, ransomware actors, scammers, thieves, and North Korean cyber actors to transfer and launder illicit proceeds.

- DOJ — Bitzlato guilty plea / illicit funds  
  https://www.justice.gov/usao-edny/pr/founder-and-majority-owner-cryptocurrency-exchange-pleads-guilty-unlicensed-money
- U.S. Treasury — 2023 DeFi Illicit Finance Risk Assessment release  
  https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1391
- U.S. Treasury — money-laundering risk assessment index  
  https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/terrorism-and-illicit-finance/money-laundering

## Mixers and obfuscation

DOJ has prosecuted cryptocurrency mixing services alleged to have processed large quantities of criminal proceeds. These cases demonstrate the tension between financial privacy and tools deliberately used to conceal crime proceeds.

- DOJ — ChipMixer takedown  
  https://www.justice.gov/usao-edpa/pr/justice-department-investigation-leads-takedown-darknet-cryptocurrency-mixer-processed
- DOJ — Samourai Wallet case  
  https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/founders-and-ceo-cryptocurrency-mixing-service-arrested-and-charged-money-laundering

# 13. Consumer and financial-stability risks

Crypto markets have experienced:

- extreme price volatility;
- exchange and lender bankruptcies;
- hacks and smart-contract exploits;
- fraud and market manipulation;
- irreversible mistaken/stolen transfers;
- stablecoin de-pegging and run risk;
- leverage and liquidation cascades;
- loss of private keys;
- regulatory uncertainty; and
- concentrated ownership/control in parts of the ecosystem.

The Federal Reserve’s post-Terra analysis notes that digital assets may offer useful innovations while still exhibiting familiar financial vulnerabilities such as runs, leverage, operational risk, opacity, and fraud.

**Primary / official:**
- Federal Reserve — Financial Stability Report, Digital Assets and Financial Stability  
  https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2022-november-financial-stability-report-funding-risks.htm
- Federal Reserve — Stablecoins in 2025  
  https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/stablecoins-in-2025-developments-and-financial-stability-implications-20260408.html

# 14. Cryptocurrency is not the same thing as blockchain

A blockchain can be public or private, permissionless or permissioned, and may operate with or without a freely traded cryptocurrency.

Examples such as supply-chain ledgers, private institutional settlement systems, and some humanitarian/audit systems should not automatically be counted as “cryptocurrency benefits” simply because they use distributed-ledger technology.

Conversely, a payment made in Bitcoin, Ether, USDC, or another crypto asset is directly relevant to cryptocurrency use.

The dataset should preserve this distinction so that benefits are not inflated by relabeling every blockchain project as a crypto success.

# 15. Bottom line

The strongest evidence-supported benefits of cryptocurrency are:

1. **Open digital ownership and peer-to-peer transfer** without a mandatory central transaction intermediary.
2. **A new globally traded asset class** that has reached multi-trillion-dollar market capitalization and entered regulated securities markets.
3. **Cross-border transfer and remittance potential**, with real use at scale in markets such as Nigeria.
4. **Financial access at the margins**, where Federal Reserve data show transactional crypto use is disproportionately higher among some unbanked/nonbank-service users.
5. **Humanitarian portability**, demonstrated by UNHCR’s USDC assistance pilot for people displaced by war in Ukraine.
6. **Programmable financial infrastructure**, especially through smart-contract networks.
7. **Digital access to dollar-denominated value** in countries where physical dollars or conventional dollar banking can be difficult to obtain.
8. **Rapidly curtailable computing load** for proof-of-work mining where grid rules deliberately reward demand response.

The strongest evidence-supported costs and risks include illicit markets, laundering, ransomware, scams, theft, volatility, consumer losses, financial-stability risks, and substantial electricity demand for proof-of-work mining.

A defensible conclusion is therefore not “crypto is good” or “crypto is bad.” It is that cryptocurrency has created real economic infrastructure and useful capabilities alongside serious externalities and criminal abuse. Public policy is strongest when it distinguishes the technology’s use cases and regulates measurable harms rather than denying either side of the record.
