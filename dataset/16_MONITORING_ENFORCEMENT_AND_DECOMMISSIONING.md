# Monitoring, Enforcement, and Decommissioning

> Project protections are only meaningful if they are measurable, reported, independently verifiable where appropriate, and connected to enforcement mechanisms.
>
> Last reviewed: 2026-10-06

## Monitoring principle

A permit or development agreement should distinguish between:

- a design promise made before construction;
- a measurable operating requirement;
- a reporting obligation;
- a test/inspection method;
- a corrective-action requirement;
- an enforcement remedy.

For example, “use low-water cooling” is less enforceable than a defined cooling design plus metering and an annual water-use limit. “Be a quiet neighbor” is less enforceable than property-line/receptor noise limits plus independent testing.

## Metrics considered for ongoing reporting

The project has considered public or City-accessible reporting of:

- water use;
- electrical demand/consumption where disclosure is lawful and not security-sensitive;
- noise-compliance tests;
- generator testing;
- emergency incidents;
- required permits and violations;
- infrastructure obligations;
- local hiring/training commitments;
- community-benefit payments;
- status of decommissioning security;
- material expansions or cooling-system changes.

Security-sensitive details should be handled consistently with Iowa public-record law and applicable exemptions rather than either disclosed indiscriminately or labeled confidential without analysis.

## October 1 company willingness to accept enforceable conditions

At the October 1 forum, Simple Mining repeatedly expressed willingness, in principle, to operate under clear enforceable conditions. Specific examples included:

- a measurable property-line noise limit;
- a water-use cap;
- restrictions limiting future expansion of the Cascade project;
- an emergency action plan;
- screening/downcast-lighting and landscaping requirements;
- decommissioning/cleanup financial assurance such as a surety bond.

These statements are useful negotiating evidence but **are not yet binding commitments**. They become enforceable only if incorporated into adopted ordinance language, permit conditions, an executed development agreement, a bond/letter of credit, or another valid legal instrument.

See [`28_SIMPLE_MINING_COMMUNITY_FORUM_2026-10-01.md`](28_SIMPLE_MINING_COMMUNITY_FORUM_2026-10-01.md).

## Independent verification

Potential verification tools include:

- applicant-funded independent engineering review before approval;
- City-approved acoustic/hydrologic/traffic reviewers;
- post-construction acceptance testing;
- scheduled recurring testing;
- complaint-triggered testing when objective criteria are met;
- inspection rights established by ordinance, permit, or development agreement;
- certified annual reports.

## Enforcement

The project-generated Cascade model ordinance proposed a menu of remedies such as permit denial, stop-work order, certificate-of-occupancy action, municipal-infraction/civil remedies, corrective studies, mitigation, operating limits, and injunctive/specific-performance remedies to the extent allowed by law.

Those remedies are model concepts, not current adopted Cascade data-center rules. Actual enforcement language should be tied to existing municipal authority and reviewed by counsel.

## Expansion and change control

A recurring concern is that a project approved for one configuration could later increase load, equipment, cooling, or physical impacts. A durable approval can define thresholds for “material expansion,” such as:

- additional MW;
- additional buildings/containers/data-hall area;
- cooling-system change;
- new generator capacity;
- water-demand increase;
- physical site expansion;
- new equipment that materially changes noise or emissions.

The project-generated model proposed no automatic vested right to future phases and renewed review of material expansion. This is a policy proposal requiring legal review.

## Decommissioning

Data centers contain high-value equipment, but closure or insolvency can still leave foundations, electrical gear, generators, tanks, cooling systems, cabling, fencing, and site restoration obligations.

A decommissioning plan can address:

1. expected useful life;
2. trigger for abandonment/inactivity;
3. equipment removal;
4. fuel/coolant/hazardous-material disposal;
5. removal or approved reuse of generators and utility equipment;
6. restoration of disturbed land;
7. protection/removal of public infrastructure;
8. estimated decommissioning cost;
9. periodic cost updates;
10. financial assurance such as bond, letter of credit, escrow, or another lawful instrument.

## Jones County comparator

Jones County's data-center ordinance is a strong nearby example because it explicitly requires a decommissioning plan, estimated cost, restoration/removal obligations, a method to ensure funds are available, and a process triggered by extended inactivity.

## Project-generated Cascade model

The June 2026 model proposed:

- a decommissioning plan before the first building permit;
- removal/reuse of obsolete equipment, generators, and fuel systems;
- hazardous-material disposal;
- land restoration;
- protection of public infrastructure;
- financial security when abandonment, insolvency, or nonuse could create a public burden;
- annual compliance reporting;
- enforcement and corrective-action authority.

Again, these are drafting proposals, not enacted City law.
