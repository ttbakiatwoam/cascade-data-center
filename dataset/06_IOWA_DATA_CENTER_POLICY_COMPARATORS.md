# Iowa Data Center Policy Comparators

> These examples are used as policy comparators. They do not automatically apply inside Cascade, and differences in county/city authority, zoning, utility service, project size, and technology matter.
>
> Last reviewed: 2026-09-10

## Linn County

Linn County adopted a data-center ordinance for **unincorporated** areas in February 2026. The county's public materials identify standards involving setbacks, noise, traffic/roads, emergency planning, site-plan review, and—for larger projects—a water study, Water Use Agreement, Economic Development Agreement, and community fund.

The approved ordinance includes unusually useful measurable noise provisions. For adjacent residential occupied structures, the ordinance uses an hourly-average limit of 55 dBA and 65 dBC; for adjacent nonresidential occupied structures, 65 dBA and 75 dBC. It also provides for acoustic review and recurring testing.

The ordinance contains road-use/restoration mechanisms and requires water-supply analysis and agreements for qualifying projects.

In July 2026 Linn County separately adopted an 18-month moratorium on new large-scale data-center rezoning applications in the EU-3 district, through January 1, 2028 unless changed earlier, to study cumulative effects including water, power, infrastructure, emergency response, and land use. This is a planning action in unincorporated Linn County, not a statewide rule.

## Jones County

Jones County Ordinance 2025-02, adopted May 13, 2025, provides another nearby Iowa comparator. Its provisions include special-use/site-plan review, applicant-funded third-party noise analysis, cooling and water documentation, electric-utility verification, emergency planning, and decommissioning.

Notable provisions identified in the project review include:

- continuous-noise standard of 60 dB daytime / 50 dB nighttime;
- a 5 dB reduction when a steady pure tone is present;
- liquid cooling and closed-loop cooling requirements;
- annual water-consumption estimate and water-source documentation;
- utility verification of maximum potential electric consumption and infrastructure sizing;
- decommissioning plan, cost estimate, restoration, and funding method;
- a restriction on cargo containers/semi-trailers as operational components, which is especially relevant when comparing Jones County policy with early reporting of a containerized Cascade proposal.

## Jackson County (working draft)

Jackson County is a nearby **draft-policy comparator**, not an adopted ordinance. The County's zoning page labels its High-Density Computing Facilities materials **“HDCF Working Drafts - For Discussion Purposes.”** As of October 6, 2026, the newest version listed by the County is dated August 17, 2026. The August 17 Zoning Commission agenda described continued review and discussion of the draft and stated that no public hearing or final recommendation on the draft ordinance was scheduled.

Because the latest County-hosted version is distributed through an Adobe shared-document link, this repository preserves a source/status record rather than transcribing language that could not be independently verified line-for-line from the current draft. An earlier June 15, 2026 copy is readable through Dubuque County and can be used for historical comparison, but it should not be presented as the August 17 version.

## City of Palo

Palo adopted Ordinance 93-2026 creating an EU-1 Data Center Exclusive Use District. The ordinance treats data centers as a principal use in the district and contemplates accessory infrastructure such as backup generation, substations/switchyards, battery storage, district-energy/microgrid systems, and water treatment/recycling. It requires major site-plan review and rezoning into the district where applicable.

## Allamakee County

Public reporting in April 2026 states that Allamakee County adopted Amendment 20 to its zoning ordinance addressing data centers. The county's approach has been cited in Iowa discussions for cooling and siting standards. Because a clean, current county-hosted final ordinance URL was not recovered during this dataset pass, details from secondary reporting should be treated as secondary evidence rather than quoted as controlling text.

## Norwalk / Project West

Norwalk's public Project West page is a useful example of infrastructure and water-capacity planning. The page describes a roughly 282-acre project area and a March 5, 2026 development agreement. Importantly, the public page distinguishes the infrastructure/development agreement from approval of an eventual data-center site plan.

The public materials describe developer responsibility for project-specific infrastructure and reserve industrial water capacity for a future large user. This is useful as a model for separating project-driven costs from existing ratepayers.

### Source

- Norwalk Iowa Southwest Development — Data Center / Project West: https://norwalkiaswdevelopment.com/datacenter

## West Des Moines / Microsoft

West Des Moines is useful as an economic-scale comparator, not a physical analogue for Cascade. A West Des Moines Chamber fact sheet, derived from City information, describes more than six million square feet of Microsoft data-center space and gives examples of annual property-tax revenue and public-infrastructure investment associated with several projects.

Those figures demonstrate that data centers can create material tax base and infrastructure investment under some agreements; they should **not** be projected onto Cascade without a project-specific assessed value, incentive package, and fiscal analysis.

### Source

- West Des Moines Chamber — Microsoft Data Centers: https://wdmchamber.org/economic-development/microsoft-data-centers/

## Comparator-use rule

Before using another project's outcome as evidence for Cascade, match the variables that materially drive the claimed impact: scale, MW, cooling, water source, site design, distance to homes, generators, utility structure, land use, climate, and enforceable permit conditions.
