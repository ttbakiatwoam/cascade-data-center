# External Policy and Technology Comparators

> These examples are useful for identifying regulatory tools and technical possibilities. They are not Iowa law and do not establish the design or impacts of the proposed Cascade project.
>
> Last reviewed: 2026-09-10

## Utah: statewide data-center policy

Utah adopted several notable data-center measures in 2026 that provide useful examples of policy categories other states are addressing.

### Executive Order 2026-03

Utah's May 29, 2026 executive order, **Establishing a Higher Bar for Data Center Development in Utah**, expressly identifies concerns involving water resources, air quality, utility rates, noise, heat, lighting, traffic, and quality of life. It also states a policy of protecting existing utility ratepayers from having new data-center energy-infrastructure costs shifted onto households and businesses.

The order is useful to this dataset because it demonstrates that a pro-development state can simultaneously pursue data-center development and stronger standards for water, infrastructure cost allocation, and local impacts.

### H.B. 76 — Data Center Water Transparency Amendments

Utah H.B. 76 established water-notification/reporting requirements for qualifying new large data centers. Among other provisions, the enrolled bill requires advance notification of anticipated water-consumption needs and reporting of estimated annual withdrawals and discharge plans.

This is a transparency model, not an Iowa requirement.

## Lehi, Utah: zoning treatment

Lehi's development code expressly defines “Data Center” in its table of uses. This is a useful example of a municipality adding the use explicitly to its zoning framework rather than relying indefinitely on analogy to another industrial/commercial use.

The broader lesson for Cascade is procedural: clear definitions and an explicit approval path reduce ambiguity for both residents and developers.

## Salt Lake County: measurable community-noise regulation

Salt Lake County Health Regulation #21 is a useful non-Iowa example of a general community-noise code with measurable receiving-property standards rather than a data-center-specific rule.

The regulation's table includes different daytime/nighttime limits by receiving-property type and additional reductions for pure-tone/repetitive-impulse sound. For example, the published regulation lists Type A single-family residential limits of 50 dBA at night and 60 dBA during the day, subject to its ambient provisions and measurement rules.

The relevance to Cascade is methodological: a noise ordinance can define receptor classes, time periods, measurement equipment, ambient adjustments, tonal penalties, and enforcement procedures.

## Flexential Fair Park: conventional data-center comparator

Flexential's Fair Park facility in Salt Lake City has been used in prior project research as an example of a conventional colocation/data-center environment. It is useful for understanding how a professionally operated server/colocation facility differs from both a hyperscale AI campus and a containerized cryptocurrency-mining site.

It should not be treated as a direct acoustic, water, or staffing analogue for Cascade without matching the design variables.

### Source

- Flexential — Salt Lake City / Fair Park Data Center: https://www.flexential.com/data-centers/ut/salt-lake-city/fair-park-data-center

## Technology-comparator rule

External examples are most useful when they demonstrate a **specific mechanism**:

- dry cooling demonstrates a technical way to limit operational water use;
- a tonal-noise standard demonstrates a way to measure an acoustic impact;
- a water-reporting statute demonstrates a transparency mechanism;
- a developer-cost rule demonstrates a ratepayer/taxpayer protection mechanism;
- an explicit zoning definition demonstrates a clearer land-use process.

They should not be used to claim that a different company's facility is technically identical to the Cascade proposal.
