# Cascade Municipal Water Baseline

> This file establishes the public baseline for Cascade's municipal water system before comparing any proposed industrial load. It does not assume that the proposed Simple Mining facility will use municipal water or any particular volume.
>
> Last reviewed: 2026-09-11

## City-published system baseline

The City of Cascade's Public Works Water Department page currently describes the municipal system as follows:

- **three groundwater wells**;
- two wells drawing from the **Jordan Sandstone Aquifer** and one from the **Silurian Limestone Aquifer**;
- approximately **1,440,000 gallons/day** of well capacity;
- a **400,000-gallon water tower**;
- approximately **230,000 gallons/day** of current municipal water consumption;
- approximately **12 miles** of underground distribution lines.

Those figures provide a useful starting point, but the difference between nominal well capacity and average daily consumption should **not** be treated automatically as available industrial capacity.

## Why nominal spare capacity is not the same as safe new-load capacity

Simple arithmetic using the City-published figures gives roughly 1.21 million gallons/day between stated well capacity and stated average consumption. That number is only a mathematical difference.

A water-system engineer would also need to account for issues such as:

- firm capacity with the largest well unavailable;
- pumping rates and well-specific limitations;
- aquifer sustainable yield;
- treatment capacity;
- storage and pressure requirements;
- peak-day and peak-hour residential/commercial demand;
- fire-flow reserve;
- drought conditions;
- emergency redundancy;
- future community growth;
- distribution-system bottlenecks;
- wastewater treatment capacity;
- the proposed facility's actual peak and annual demand.

Therefore, project approval should rely on a water-capacity study rather than simply subtracting average use from total nameplate well capacity.

## Iowa DNR source-water information

Iowa DNR's Source Water Protection Tracker identifies Cascade Municipal Water Supply as a community groundwater system and provides well/aquifer records. The public tracker lists active, standby, unused, and plugged wells and identifies Cambrian-Ordovician and Silurian sources in the system's history.

Because well status and system configuration can change, the City and DNR's current operating records should control over older historical entries.

## Water quality

Cascade publishes annual Consumer Confidence / Water Quality Reports. The current City page links reports for 2025, 2024, 2023, and earlier years. The 2025 report is the current public report linked from that page as of this dataset review.

A data-center water analysis should distinguish **water quantity/capacity** from **drinking-water quality**. A facility can create a capacity concern without contaminating drinking water, and a water-quality report does not by itself establish how much new industrial demand the system can safely support.

# Cascade municipal swimming-pool comparator

A familiar local water use can make otherwise abstract gallon figures easier to understand, but the comparison has to use compatible measurements.

## What is publicly documented

Burbach Aquatics, the aquatic engineering firm associated with the replacement pool, identifies the Cascade facility as a **2020** project with **6,015 square feet of pool water surface**. Its project description includes a zero-depth entry area, toddler slide and spray features, a six-lane competition pool, a larger slide, shade structures, and two diving boards.

The City of Cascade's current swimming-pool page independently describes the operating facility as having a zero-entry area, water features, two diving boards (one meter and three meters), and a larger youth slide.

The City's June 24, 2019 Council minutes document Resolution #38-19 approving the notice to proceed and construction contract for Contracts #1 and #2 of the **Cascade Municipal Swimming Pool Project with Ricchio, Inc.** The May 28, 2019 minutes document authorization of **$1.965 million in general-obligation swimming-pool bonds**.

Iowa Community Attraction and Tourism program records identify the Cascade project as **“Wave of the Future,”** with a total project cost of approximately **$3.389 million** and a state CAT award of approximately $229,000. Iowa DNR's Land and Water Conservation Fund material separately describes the replacement-pool scope, including zero-depth entry, six 25-yard lap lanes, diving towers, a small slide, water features, and accessibility improvements.

## Pool volume: authoritative figure still pending

The exact **gallon capacity of the pool** has not yet been recovered from the publicly indexed copy of the 2019 construction/bid documents during this research pass.

The 6,015-square-foot water-surface figure is verified, but surface area alone is **not enough to calculate an authoritative pool volume** because the zero-depth section, competition lanes, diving well, transitions, gutters, surge capacity, and varying depths materially affect the result.

The preferred authoritative source remains the original Burbach/Ricchio pool plan, hydraulic schedule, equipment schedule, or bid specification approved during the 2019 construction process.

The following section nevertheless performs the **raw arithmetic requested for comparison purposes**. Every number derived from an assumed depth or assumed evaporation rate is labeled as an estimate and must not be represented as a Cascade Municipal Utilities meter reading.

## Pool water consumption: do not confuse it with pool volume

Even after the exact gallon capacity is recovered, that number will describe the amount of water contained in the pool when filled—not its annual or daily water consumption.

A municipal pool normally recirculates and treats the same body of water. Additional municipal water may be required for:

- initial/seasonal filling;
- evaporation replacement;
- splash-out and swimmer displacement;
- filter backwashing;
- cleaning and maintenance;
- leaks or repairs;
- intentional partial draining/refilling; and
- domestic uses in showers, toilets, concessions, and bathhouse facilities.

No sufficiently documented Cascade pool meter dataset or annual seasonal gallon total has yet been recovered. The calculations below are therefore **mathematical scenarios only**.

# Raw mathematical estimate of Cascade pool water use

> **IMPORTANT: THIS IS NOT METERED CASCADE WATER-USAGE DATA.** These calculations have no direct basis in Cascade Municipal Utilities meter recordings. They use the verified 6,015-square-foot pool surface, standard volume conversion, the City's published 2026 operating dates, and explicitly stated assumptions. Actual City meter records should replace these estimates as soon as they are obtained.

## 1. Basic conversion: one inch of water across the Cascade pool

Known water surface:

**6,015 square feet**

One inch equals 1/12 of a foot, and one cubic foot contains approximately 7.48052 U.S. gallons.

Therefore:

**6,015 sq. ft. × (1/12 ft.) × 7.48052 gal/cu. ft. = approximately 3,750 gallons**

So every one-inch change in average water level across the entire 6,015-square-foot surface represents approximately **3,750 gallons**.

That conversion is geometry. It does not depend on the pool's total depth.

## 2. Illustrative pool-capacity estimate from assumed average depth

Because the exact hydraulic volume has not yet been recovered, the following uses three deliberately simple assumed average depths:

| Assumed average depth | Raw calculated volume | Share of City's stated 230,000 gal/day average use | Share of 400,000-gal water-tower storage | Share of 1.44M gal/day nominal well capacity |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4.0 ft | ~179,981 gal | 78.3% | 45.0% | 12.5% |
| 4.5 ft | ~202,479 gal | 88.0% | 50.6% | 14.1% |
| 5.0 ft | ~224,977 gal | 97.8% | 56.2% | 15.6% |

Formula:

**6,015 sq. ft. × assumed average depth × 7.48052 gallons/cu. ft.**

EPA WaterSense's commercial-pool guidance lists a generic public in-ground pool example at 4,000 square feet, 5 feet deep, and about 150,000 gallons. That EPA example is useful only as a reasonableness reference; it is **not** a measurement of Cascade's pool. Cascade's zero-depth entry and diving area make its actual average depth facility-specific.

A practical midpoint for discussion only is an assumed **4.5-foot average depth**, producing a mathematical capacity estimate of approximately **202,479 gallons**.

Again: **202,479 gallons is an estimate, not an official Cascade pool capacity.**

## 3. 2026 pool season

The City of Cascade's pool page states:

- **Opening:** Monday, May 25, 2026
- **Closing:** Labor Day, Monday, September 7, 2026, after the 1–5 p.m. open swim

Counting both opening and closing dates gives **106 calendar days** in the scheduled 2026 operating season.

This does not mean the pool was continuously open to swimmers for all 106 days or that there were no weather, staffing, maintenance, swim-meet, or other closures. It is simply the City's published seasonal date span.

## 4. Illustrative evaporation-replacement scenarios for the 2026 season

Because actual evaporation depends on water temperature, air temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation, pool covers, operating hours, water features, and other conditions, this project does **not** claim a measured Cascade evaporation rate.

For transparent raw math, three water-level-loss scenarios are shown below:

| Assumed average evaporation / net surface loss | Calculated replacement per day | 106-day calculated total |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.25 inch/day | ~937 gal/day | ~99,365 gal |
| 0.375 inch/day | ~1,406 gal/day | ~149,047 gal |
| 0.50 inch/day | ~1,875 gal/day | ~198,729 gal |

Formula:

**3,749.6 gallons per inch × assumed inches/day × 106 days**

These are intentionally simple scenarios, **not a reconstruction of 2026 weather**. Rainfall could reduce municipal makeup-water demand. Covers could reduce evaporation substantially. Conversely, heated water, wind, water features, splash-out, backwashing, cleaning, leakage, bathhouse use, or other operations could increase total metered facility consumption.

EPA WaterSense identifies evaporation as the largest component of pool water loss in its commercial-pool guidance and notes that evaporation varies with temperature, ambient conditions, convection/wind, and exposed surface area. EPA also recommends dedicated water metering as the accurate way to monitor actual pool water use and detect abnormal losses.

## 5. Estimated 2026 total under a “one full fill + evaporation” scenario

If—and this is a major assumption—the pool required approximately one complete seasonal fill from municipal water before opening, a simple total can be calculated by adding the hypothetical fill volume to the illustrative evaporation replacement.

### Low scenario

- assumed 4.0-ft average-depth fill: **~179,981 gallons**
- assumed 0.25-inch/day evaporation replacement for 106 days: **~99,365 gallons**
- calculated total: **~279,346 gallons**
- season-average equivalent: **~2,635 gallons/day**

### Midpoint scenario

- assumed 4.5-ft average-depth fill: **~202,479 gallons**
- assumed 0.375-inch/day evaporation replacement for 106 days: **~149,047 gallons**
- calculated total: **~351,526 gallons**
- season-average equivalent: **~3,316 gallons/day**

### High illustrative scenario

- assumed 5.0-ft average-depth fill: **~224,977 gallons**
- assumed 0.50-inch/day evaporation replacement for 106 days: **~198,729 gallons**
- calculated total: **~423,706 gallons**
- season-average equivalent: **~3,997 gallons/day**

Therefore, a transparent mathematical range for **one assumed full fill plus evaporation replacement during the published 2026 season is approximately 279,000–424,000 gallons**, with a midpoint scenario around **352,000 gallons**.

### What this number does NOT include

The estimate above does not affirmatively add water for:

- filter backwashing;
- splash-out and drag-out;
- water-feature losses;
- intentional draining or water replacement for chemistry control;
- cleaning;
- leaks;
- showers;
- toilets;
- concessions;
- landscaping; or
- any other facility use.

It also does not subtract precipitation that entered the pool or account for any cover-related savings.

For those reasons, the estimate should **never** be quoted as “Cascade pool used 352,000 gallons in 2026.” The accurate statement is:

> **Using the verified 6,015-square-foot surface area, the City's 106-day 2026 season, an assumed 4.5-foot average depth, and an assumed 0.375-inch/day surface loss, raw arithmetic produces an illustrative one-fill-plus-evaporation estimate of about 352,000 gallons. No Cascade meter record has yet been used to validate that figure.**

## 6. Why the pool is a useful local comparator

The pool operates during the part of the year when outdoor evaporation, heat, and recreational water demand are most relevant. It is therefore a useful **local peak-season comparator** for discussing the scale of municipal water demand.

It may be one of Cascade's larger seasonal municipal-water users, but that has **not been established by customer-level meter records**. Until the City provides comparative usage data, the repository should describe that as a hypothesis rather than a fact.

The value of the comparison is that the pool can provide a real local benchmark for:

- how much water a major City-operated facility actually receives during summer;
- daily and monthly peak-season use;
- how initial fill differs from continuing makeup-water demand;
- how much demand is caused by evaporation versus filtration/backwash and other operations; and
- what scale of peak-season demand Cascade's existing utility system has historically supplied.

Historical pool consumption does **not by itself establish a formal City policy defining an “acceptable” daily industrial water-use level**. It can, however, show the magnitude of water demand the City has actually served and managed at a public facility during summer peak conditions.

## 7. Meter records to request at the first data-center committee meeting

At the first meeting of the data-center committee, the project recommends requesting the underlying City records needed to replace these estimates with evidence.

Because Burbach identifies the current replacement pool as a **2020** facility, the clean historical request is for the **seven operating seasons from 2020 through 2026 inclusive**. If records for the predecessor pool remain available, 2019 and earlier data should also be requested as a before/after comparison.

Requested records should include, where available:

1. daily or highest-resolution available municipal-water meter readings for the swimming-pool property for each 2020–2026 season;
2. monthly billed or internal utility consumption totals;
3. initial spring fill dates and gallons supplied;
4. closing/draining procedures and estimated discharge volume;
5. meter identification and whether one meter serves the pool, bathhouse, concessions, irrigation, or other uses together;
6. automatic-fill or makeup-water records;
7. filter-backwash schedules and estimated backwash volume;
8. known leaks, repairs, or abnormal-use events;
9. pool operating dates for each season;
10. any engineering document stating the pool's designed hydraulic gallon capacity;
11. any City analysis of peak-day water demand associated with the pool; and
12. if available in an appropriate public or anonymized form, comparison with other high-volume municipal-water accounts so the pool's relative ranking can be established rather than assumed.

The purpose of obtaining seven seasons of records is to establish an empirical local baseline across hot, cool, wet, and dry summers. That will be much more useful than relying on one estimated season or on a generalized national pool benchmark.

## How the pool comparison should be reported when authoritative records are recovered

Once an authoritative pool-volume figure and meter history are located, useful scale comparisons can include:

- **pool capacity ÷ 230,000 gallons/day** = how many average current city-use days equal one complete pool fill;
- **pool capacity ÷ 1,440,000 gallons/day** = one complete fill as a share of the City's stated nominal daily well capacity;
- **pool capacity ÷ 400,000 gallons** = pool volume relative to stated water-tower storage;
- **actual pool seasonal consumption ÷ number of operating days** = documented daily seasonal average;
- **highest pool-use day ÷ 230,000 gallons/day** = peak pool use relative to the City's currently stated average system-wide day; and
- **pool seasonal consumption ÷ city seasonal consumption** = the pool's documented share of municipal water use during the same period.

These remain scale comparisons. A one-time seasonal fill, seasonal recreational operation, and a 24/7 industrial demand have different demand profiles and should not be treated as operationally identical.

Before assigning a water impact to the proposed facility, obtain:

1. whether municipal water will be used at all;
2. average gallons/day;
3. peak hot-weather gallons/day;
4. annual gallons/year;
5. fire-suppression demand;
6. construction water demand;

10. City/utility engineering confirmation that the proposed demand can be served without impairing existing customers or required reserves.

## Comparison with other uses

Earlier project research considered comparing the proposed facility with familiar uses such as greenhouses, irrigation, public facilities, construction, and other local water demands. Such comparisons are only useful when they use the same metric:

- gross withdrawal vs. consumptive use;
- average day vs. peak day;
- annual total;
- potable vs. non-potable water;
- returned wastewater vs. evaporated water;
- seasonal vs. 24/7 demand.

For example, university extension guidance shows that greenhouse peak irrigation demand can be large on a per-acre basis, while a fully dry closed-loop data-center heat-rejection system can use relatively little operational cooling water. Conversely, an evaporatively cooled data center can use much more water. The actual design must control the comparison.
