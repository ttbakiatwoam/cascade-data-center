# Agriculture, Greenhouses, and Land Use

> This file preserves the project's land-use and agricultural comparisons without framing agriculture and data centers as mutually exclusive industries.
>
> Last reviewed: 2026-09-10

## Iowa agricultural context

The 2022 Census of Agriculture reports approximately 25.9 million acres of cropland in Iowa, including about 12.6 million acres of corn harvested for grain and about 9.5 million acres of soybeans. Agriculture is therefore central to Iowa's economy, land use, water quality, and community identity.

USDA Economic Research Service data also show that U.S. corn is not used solely as direct human food. Major uses include livestock feed, ethanol, exports, and food/seed/industrial products. This matters when making “food versus technology” land-use claims: the actual food-system relationship is more complex than acreage alone.

## Data centers and farmland

A data center converts a relatively small physical footprint into a high electrical/compute load, while row-crop agriculture occupies much larger areas with very different environmental effects. Neither is impact-free. Relevant comparison categories include:

- land permanently converted or disturbed;
- soil erosion and runoff;
- fertilizer/nutrient losses;
- water withdrawal and water quality;
- electricity/fuel consumption;
- greenhouse-gas emissions;
- stormwater behavior;
- local tax value and employment;
- long-term reversibility/decommissioning.

The project has rejected simplistic national acreage extrapolations as a substitute for a site-specific Cascade analysis.

## Iowa nutrient and soil-loss context

The Iowa Nutrient Reduction Strategy establishes a statewide goal of reducing annual nitrogen and phosphorus loads by 45%. It addresses both point and nonpoint sources. Agricultural nutrient runoff is therefore a documented environmental issue in Iowa and is relevant when comparing alternative land uses, but it does not excuse environmental impacts from industrial development.

Iowa State University's STRIPS research demonstrates that relatively small prairie-strip areas integrated into row-crop fields can sharply reduce sediment and nutrient movement in experimental settings. This provides evidence that agricultural environmental impacts are manageable through design and conservation practices rather than an argument for eliminating agriculture.

## Greenhouses as an alternative industrial/agricultural use

Greenhouses and controlled-environment agriculture have also been discussed as possible economic-development alternatives. Their impacts must be quantified rather than assumed.

University Extension guidance shows that greenhouse irrigation can require substantial peak water capacity. UMass Extension gives a rule of thumb of roughly 0.3–0.4 gallons per square foot of growing area per day on peak warm days. University of Arkansas guidance gives a broad rule of roughly 1–2 quarts per square foot per day, or approximately 11,000–22,000 gallons/day per acre of growing space, before evaporative-cooling and other non-crop uses.

These are **design rules of thumb**, not a prediction for a particular greenhouse. Crop, season, irrigation recapture, hydroponic design, climate, glazing, cooling, and water reuse can change consumption dramatically.

This is the same methodological lesson applied to data centers: compare a defined facility design against another defined facility design rather than using an industry label.

## Hydroponic / controlled-environment production

Hydroponic systems can recirculate nutrient solution and allow year-round local food production, but plants still transpire water and facilities require climate control. University of Florida guidance notes that mature greenhouse tomato/cucumber plants can use roughly 2–2.5 quarts per plant per day during peak harvest under the described conditions.

A Choose Iowa profile documents year-round hydroponic greens and tomato production in nearby Dyersville, demonstrating that controlled-environment agriculture is already part of the regional economy. This is contextual evidence, not a proposed replacement project for the Cascade site.

## Comparison discipline

A greenhouse-vs-data-center comparison should specify, for each alternative:

1. acres and enclosed square footage;
2. peak and annual water use;
3. water source;
4. wastewater or nutrient discharge;
5. power and fuel demand;
6. noise and lighting;
7. traffic;
8. employment and wages;
9. tax treatment;
10. environmental mitigation;
11. lifecycle/decommissioning.

Without those variables, a daily-water-use comparison can be misleading in either direction.
