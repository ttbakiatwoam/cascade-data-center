# Cascade Zoning and Local Authority

> This is policy/legal research, not legal advice. Exact enforceability should be reviewed by the City Attorney against the current official ordinance text and Iowa law.
>
> Last reviewed: 2026-10-06

## Cascade zoning record

Cascade's current online Code of Ordinances does not reproduce the full 1992 zoning ordinance. Chapter 11 states that Ordinance 260-92 and its amendments were specifically saved from repeal and remain in force. The chapter's amendment history includes Ordinance 265-93 for M-2 District Regulations and later amendments, including Ordinance 05-22 affecting C-1, C-2, M-1, and M-2 regulations.

That means a reliable determination of whether a particular data-center design is permitted, conditionally permitted, or requires a zoning change should be made from the underlying zoning ordinance and amendments, not from an assumption based solely on the abbreviated online index.

The searchable Chapter 11 index reviewed for this project did not itself contain a definition of “data center.”

## Site-specific zoning record — October 6, 2026

The City of Cascade ArcGIS zoning map identifies the Simple Mining site area as **Heavy Industrial, M-2**. The selected zoning feature displays **PIN 1932127004** and reports a July 19, 2022 last-edit date.

Dubuque County's current parcel record identifies the property instead as **PIN 1932176027, 113 Industrial St SE, Lot 2 Cascade Industrial Park 14th Addition**, containing **15.22 acres** and owned by **Simple Mining LLC**. The most supportable working interpretation is that the zoning layer retains an older/predecessor PIN while the county record reflects the later/current parcel configuration.

This mismatch does **not** by itself establish a zoning change. For this research dataset, the City ArcGIS map supports the **M-2 Heavy Industrial** district designation, while Dubuque County controls the current parcel identity, acreage, ownership, sale, and assessment facts. Whether a final data-center application is permitted, conditional, or requires another approval remains a separate legal question under the controlling ordinance text.

## Building-permit authority

Cascade's current code requires a City Council permit before a structure is erected, reconstructed, altered, or added to. It also states that the City Council has authority to accept or reject submitted plans and specifications. The City has a standard building-permit form requiring project description, legal/site information, setbacks, and a drawing showing existing/proposed structures and property-line distances.

These provisions should be read together with the saved zoning ordinance and current state building-code requirements.

## Iowa municipal home rule

Iowa Code §364.1 gives cities home-rule power, subject to constitutional and statutory limitations, to protect the rights, property, and residents of the city and to preserve and improve peace, safety, health, welfare, comfort, and convenience.

## Iowa zoning authority

Iowa Code Chapter 414 authorizes municipal zoning. Section 414.1 permits cities to regulate and restrict matters including the size and location of buildings and the use of buildings, structures, and land for trade, industry, residence, and other purposes, subject to state-law limits. Sections 414.2 and 414.3 provide for zoning districts and comprehensive-plan considerations.

## Strongest local-regulation categories

The research project has treated the following as the strongest candidates for locally enforceable, physically measurable standards, subject to legal review:

- setbacks and site design;
- noise at property lines and sensitive receptors;
- cooling/water-use documentation;
- stormwater and drainage;
- roads and construction traffic;
- screening and exterior lighting;
- fire/emergency plans;
- generator location/testing/noise where not preempted;
- infrastructure-cost responsibility through lawful agreements;
- reporting, inspection, and compliance mechanisms;
- decommissioning and financial assurance;
- conditions tied to material expansion or changes that increase physical impacts.

## Workload/customer restrictions require caution

A recurring project question has been whether zoning should dictate who a private facility may serve or exactly what lawful computing workload may run inside it. Iowa zoning law clearly reaches land use and physical development, but a restriction aimed primarily at customers, tenants, or data-processing content is legally different from regulating noise, water, structures, roads, or other land-use impacts.

Accordingly, any tenant-mix, cryptocurrency percentage, AI-use, or customer restriction should receive specific legal review rather than being assumed valid. A more durable regulatory approach may be to define the maximum physical operating envelope regardless of customer: MW, water, noise, cooling, generator emissions, traffic, setbacks, and buildout.
