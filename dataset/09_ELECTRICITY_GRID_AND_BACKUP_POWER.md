# Electricity, Grid Impacts, and Backup Power

> National data-center electricity forecasts describe the sector as a whole. They do not establish the load of the proposed Cascade facility.
>
> Last reviewed: 2026-10-06

## National context

The U.S. Department of Energy and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory have documented rapid growth in data-center electricity demand. The 2024 LBNL report estimated U.S. data centers used about 176 TWh in 2023, roughly 4.4% of U.S. electricity, and projected substantially higher demand by 2028 under multiple scenarios.

A June 2026 LBNL update extended the analysis toward 2030 and estimated a central case around 11.8% of U.S. electricity, with a broad scenario range. These projections illustrate why utilities and regulators increasingly focus on interconnection, generation, transmission, rate design, and cost allocation for very large loads.

## Why national numbers do not answer the Cascade question

A data center's local electrical impact depends on:

- initial and maximum MW;
- ramp/phase schedule;
- load factor and operating profile;
- curtailability and demand response;
- substation and feeder capacity;
- required transmission/distribution upgrades;
- utility ownership and tariff structure;
- dedicated vs. shared infrastructure;
- who pays capital and ongoing costs;
- backup generation and UPS/BESS configuration.

At the October 1, 2026 forum, both Simple Mining and the Maquoketa Valley Electric Cooperative representative described the current Cascade concept as approximately **40 MW**. This is now a project-specific representation, but the final maximum demand should still be verified from the executed utility/interconnection documents.

## Simple Mining operating context

Simple Mining advertises more than 150 MW under management across its Iowa hosting fleet. That provides company-scale context but should not be used to infer the proposed Cascade site's load. The company should provide the site's actual design demand by phase and at maximum buildout.

## October 1 utility representations

The Maquoketa Valley Electric Cooperative representative made several project-specific statements at the forum:

- no Cascade service agreement had yet been executed/signed as of October 1;
- the current project concept was about **40 MW**;
- a capacity study performed with Central Iowa Power Cooperative was described as finding adequate transmission capacity for that load while reserving capacity for ordinary/native growth;
- Simple Mining would be expected to pay **100% of project-driven transmission, substation, and distribution upgrades upfront**, subject to a later true-up;
- a load of this size would be required to bring/add **new generation** rather than consume generation reserved for existing/native load;
- if structured as described, the utility expected the project to have a neutral or positive impact on other cooperative members' rates.

These are utility representations at the forum, not substitutes for the underlying study, tariff, generation arrangement, or executed service/interconnection agreement.

## Ratepayer protection

A recurring policy principle in this project is that existing residents and businesses should not be forced to subsidize infrastructure primarily required by a new large load without an explicit public-policy justification.

Utah's 2026 Executive Order 2026-03 is a useful external policy example because it expressly states a goal of protecting utility ratepayers from shifting new data-center energy-infrastructure costs onto existing households and businesses. Utah's law is not controlling in Iowa, but the cost-allocation principle is relevant to negotiation and utility review.

Norwalk's Project West materials offer an Iowa comparator in which project-specific infrastructure costs are assigned to the developer under the development arrangement.

## Demand response and curtailment

Cryptocurrency-mining loads can sometimes be more interruptible than conventional cloud/enterprise loads. Whether the Cascade proposal could or would participate in demand-response or curtailment programs is an open project-specific question. Any claimed grid benefit should be supported by the actual utility tariff/agreement rather than assumed from the workload category.

## Backup generation, UPS, and batteries

Backup-power systems can create separate local impacts from normal grid consumption:

- generator count and MW;
- diesel or natural-gas fuel storage;
- air permits and emissions;
- testing schedules and noise;
- emergency runtime;
- spill containment;
- battery/UPS chemistry and capacity;
- fire-response requirements.

At the October 1 forum, Simple Mining estimated approximately **15 backup generators** and later identified **diesel** as the planned fuel. The company described them as outage/backup equipment rather than normal primary generation and said they would be enclosed and subject to state air permitting. The exact models, aggregate MW, fuel storage, runtime, emissions, and testing cadence remained unresolved; when asked for a testing frequency, the presenter deferred rather than giving a firm schedule.

The community-question bank still seeks the final number, type, size, emissions, testing schedule, noise, and UPS/BESS configuration.

## Recommended Cascade evidence

A project record should ideally include:

1. utility-confirmed initial and maximum load;
2. single-line electrical/site diagrams appropriate for public release;
3. interconnection/substation/transmission requirements;
4. cost-responsibility agreement;
5. expected demand profile and load factor;
6. demand-response/curtailment provisions, if any;
7. generator and fuel plan;
8. UPS/BESS plan;
9. air/noise permitting information;
10. expansion thresholds requiring renewed review.
