# Iowa homes built after 2015: energy-code construction and noise reduction

> **Research note — November 3, 2026.** This is evidence for the proposed Cascade M-2 noise ordinance, **not enacted zoning law**, not an acoustic certification of any dwelling, and not a legal opinion. Building age does not establish a fixed decibel reduction.

## 1. What building code actually applied after 2015?

Iowa did **not** adopt the *2015 edition* of the International Energy Conservation Code (IECC) merely because a home was built after 2015. Iowa's statewide residential energy baseline is the **2012 IECC with Iowa amendments**, effective **April 1, 2014**, and it remained the listed Iowa state residential energy code in 2026.[^1][^2]

Iowa Code §103A.8A requires energy-conservation requirements for new one- and two-family construction. Under City of Cascade Code §6-12-8, **Ordinance 02-22 (August 22, 2022)** adopted the **current State of Iowa Building Code** by reference for construction within the city; the older period requires checking the code actually effective at the time of each home's permit.[^2][^3] The separate International Residential Code (IRC) is a residential **building** code; its edition is not automatically the applicable IECC **energy** code.

These are **minimum energy requirements, not noise-insulation specifications**. The examined statewide residential energy provisions do **not** prescribe a single exterior-wall or window **STC, OITC, dBA, or dBC** sound reduction that can be assigned to every Iowa home built after 2015.

## 2. Envelope features likely to reduce sound incidentally

The 2012 IECC's residential prescriptive envelope path, with Iowa amendments, establishes these relevant features. An approved alternative code-compliance path or different assembly may yield a different construction; the entries are **not a guarantee of the materials installed in a particular house**.[^2][^4]

| Feature | Iowa post-2014 residential energy provision | Acoustic significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wood-frame exterior walls | Typical prescriptive path: **R-20 cavity insulation, or R-13 cavity plus R-5 continuous insulation** for climate zones 5 and 6 under Iowa's amendment. | Insulation can absorb part of the airborne sound energy, but thermal **R-value is not a sound-transmission rating**. |
| Exterior air leakage | **No more than 4 ACH50**, measured by blower-door testing at a **50-Pascal pressure difference**, with Iowa's amendment of the model 3-ACH50 value. | Air sealing can reduce sound paths through cracks and gaps. **4 ACH50 is a test condition, not the number of air changes during everyday occupancy.** |
| Windows and doors | The energy code regulates **thermal performance**, plus air leakage and envelope sealing. | Closed and weatherstripped windows often reduce incoming sound; thermal U-factor alone does **not** reveal an acoustic rating. |
| Roof and other assemblies | Applicable insulation and envelope provisions vary by assembly and compliance path. | Roof design, attic vents, mechanical penetrations, and construction details can dominate actual sound transmission. |

Climate-zone context: **Dubuque County is IECC Zone 5A; Delaware County is Zone 6A** under the county mapping associated with the 2012-code era.[^5] This matters when comparing houses around Cascade across the county boundary.

## 3. FHWA planning estimates for exterior-to-interior traffic noise

The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) *Noise Measurement Handbook*, Table 6-1, reports the following approximate **outdoor-to-indoor noise reduction (OINR)** factors. They are **traffic-noise planning assumptions**, not guarantees based on house age or required Iowa sound-isolation values.[^6]

| Building type / window condition | Illustrative exterior-to-interior reduction |
| --- | ---: |
| Any building, windows **open** | 10 dB |
| Light-frame building, ordinary windows **closed** | **20 dB** |
| Light-frame building, **storm windows** | **25 dB** |
| Masonry building, **single glazing** | **25 dB** |
| Masonry building, **double glazing** | **35 dB** |

**Illustration only:** An exterior level of **65 dBA**, minus a **hypothetical** 20 dB reduction, equals **about 45 dBA indoors**. The subtraction is appropriate only when the exterior noise spectrum, measurement geometry, and the house's **actual** reduction support that estimate. It cannot prove compliance with the M-2 ordinance. In addition, the M-2 **property-line** ceiling remains enforceable regardless of indoor insulation.

**Critical limitation for data centers:** FHWA's planning factors concern transportation-noise spectra. Industrial cooling fans, transformers, generator engines, and computing facilities can create relatively strong **low-frequency** sounds. An A-weighted traffic-noise reduction **cannot be treated as a 20 dBC or 25 dBC reduction**. Low-frequency noise may penetrate walls and glazing differently, cause resonance, and need measurement by frequency band.[^6][^7]

## 4. Implications for Cascade's proposed indoor-receptor standard

The working ordinance's proposed **§165.11(G)(4)(b)** places the receiver **inside an occupied room on the sound-source side of the building**, with **doors and windows closed** and the microphone within **five feet of the nearest source-facing exterior wall**. That is a **proposed local location requirement**, not a prescriptive state acoustic code. The numerical limits remain **55 dBA/65 dBC daytime** and **50 dBA/60 dBC nighttime** measured as **Leq,1h**.

1. **Do not automatically subtract 20 dB or 35 dB** from a developer's outdoor prediction based on the home being built after 2015.
2. When feasible and lawfully authorized, use **paired outdoor and indoor measurements at a representative occupied room**, recording window/door positions, mechanical ventilation, source operation, indoor background, and instrument locations.
3. Document **frequency-dependent facade attenuation**, including A- and C-weighted levels and appropriate one-third-octave data for low-frequency noise. Identify any measured versus modeled values.
4. If occupants decline entry, explain the limitations of an indoor model. **Do not describe the predicted result as a verified indoor compliance measurement**.
5. Ask an independent acoustician whether **50 dBA Leq,1h indoors at night** is sufficiently protective of sleep. The WHO community-noise guidance discusses **30 dBA Leq indoors in bedrooms at night** as a sleep-related guideline; that guidance is **not Iowa law**.[^8]
6. Have the City Attorney examine whether an indoor-only requirement creates enforceability or access problems and whether a separate exterior outdoor-use-area limit should be retained.

**Practical conclusion:** Post-2015 Iowa residential code compliance provides a basis for expecting **insulated, air-sealed building envelopes**, not a documented **minimum 20 dB, 25 dB, or 35 dB acoustic reduction** for any individual home.
