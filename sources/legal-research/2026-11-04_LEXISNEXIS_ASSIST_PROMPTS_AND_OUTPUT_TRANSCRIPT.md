Query
You are a part of city council working on drafting city ordinance around data centers. How
would you go about defining a data center?



Response
For a city ordinance, I'd define a data center by what it does and how big it is, not by what it's
called. A good definition sweeps in large server facilities that strain the power grid, water supply,
and neighbors, and leaves out the server closets and small IT rooms found in ordinary offices,
hospitals, and schools. A definition with three parts does that: a functional core, a size threshold,
and a list of exclusions.

1. Functional core
Start with the building's main purpose:
"Data center" means a building, group of buildings, or portion of a building whose primary use
is to house computer servers, data storage, networking equipment, and associated power and
cooling infrastructure, for the purpose of storing, processing, or transmitting electronic data,
whether for the owner's own use or for third parties.
Some points to settle:
     Primary use: Requiring that servers be the primary use keeps out offices that happen to have
      server rooms.
     Who it serves: Cover all business models, including self-built company facilities, rented
      ("colocation") space, cloud providers, AI computing, and crypto mining. Leave crypto
      mining out only if you plan to regulate it separately.
     "Campus" language: Without it, a developer could split one project into several smaller
      buildings, each under the threshold.

A common approach uses power as the main trigger and floor area as a backup. For example, the
ordinance could apply to facilities with a total power demand of [X] MW or more, or [Y] square
feet or more of floor area used for data center equipment. You can also set tiers, such as "small"
and "large" data centers, with different zoning treatment for each, for instance allowed by right in
industrial zones versus requiring a special or conditional use permit.

3. Exclusions
State plainly what is not covered, for example:
   Accessory server rooms that serve the main use on the same site and stay under the threshold.
   Telecommunications equipment such as cell sites and central offices, if other code sections
    already regulate it.
   Equipment owned by government or public utilities, if appropriate.
   Small "edge" computing cabinets in the public right-of-way.

4. Related terms to define alongside it
   "IT load" or "critical load", so the megawatt threshold can be measured.
   "Backup generator" or "on-site power generation", including batteries and gas turbines.
   "Cooling system", separating closed-loop systems from evaporative ones that consume
    water.
   "Expansion" or "modification", so adding capacity later triggers review again.
   "Hyperscale data center", if you want a separate tier for the largest facilities.

5. Practical drafting tips
   Match the definition to what the ordinance does. If it covers zoning, noise, water, and
    taxes, check that one definition works for all of them, or create sub-definitions.
   Avoid naming specific technologies, such as particular chip types or "AI," so the definition
    doesn't go out of date.
   Require applicants to disclose planned power demand, water use, and generator capacity,
    so staff can tell whether the threshold is met.
   Check for conflicts with state law. Some states have laws on data center tax incentives,
    utility rates, or limits on local zoning power that may define the term or restrict what the city
    can do.
   Ask your local electric and water utilities for input before choosing threshold numbers.

Definition of "Data Center" Under Iowa Law
Iowa law provides a specific definition of "data center" in the context of sales and use tax
exemptions. [1] Under Iowa Code § 423.4, a "data center business" is defined as a business that
meets certain investment and operational criteria. [2] Specifically, the business must make an
investment in an Iowa physical location within the first three years of operation or lease term,
with the investment amounting to at least $1 million but not exceeding $10 million for a newly
constructed building or $5 million for a rehabilitated building. [3] The statute also outlines that
the refund applies to state sales and use tax paid on fuel and electricity used in the operation of
the data center, but excludes local option sales and services taxes. [1] [4] Additionally, Iowa
Code § 427.1 provides exemptions for property used by data center businesses, including
computers, cooling systems, power infrastructure, and other equipment necessary for the
maintenance and operation of the data center. [5]

Authority and Limits of Iowa Cities Under Home Rule

General Home Rule Authority
Under Article III, Section 38A of the Iowa Constitution and Iowa Code § 364.1, cities in Iowa
are granted broad home rule authority to regulate local affairs and government, provided such
regulations are not inconsistent with state law. [6] [7] However, cities are expressly prohibited
from levying taxes unless authorized by the General Assembly. [8] [9] [10] This authority allows
cities to regulate matters of local concern, but any ordinance that conflicts with state law or
invades an area reserved to the legislature is preempted. [11] [12]

Preemption of Local Ordinances
Preemption can be express or implied. [13] Express preemption occurs when the legislature
explicitly prohibits local regulation in a specific area. [14] [15] Implied preemption arises when a
local ordinance conflicts with state law or when the legislature demonstrates an intent to occupy
a regulatory field entirely. [16] [17] For example, a local ordinance is preempted if it prohibits an
act permitted by state law or permits an act prohibited by state law. [18] [19] [20] In the context
of data centers, any local regulation must not conflict with the state’s statutory framework
governing data center businesses, such as the tax exemptions and property classifications under
Iowa Code § 423.3, Iowa Code § 423.4, and. [4] [5] [21]

Authority and Limits of Iowa Cities Under Zoning Laws

Zoning Powers
Iowa Code § 414.1 grants cities the authority to regulate land use through zoning ordinances to
promote the health, safety, and general welfare of the community. [22] [23] This includes
regulating the location and use of buildings and land for trade, industry, and other purposes. [23]
[24] Cities may amend zoning ordinances as circumstances warrant, provided such amendments
are not unreasonable or capricious. [25] [26]

Restrictions on Zoning Authority
Cities are limited in their zoning authority by state law. [22] [27] For example, Iowa Code §
414.1(1)(b) prohibits cities from adopting regulations that discriminate based on familial or
nonfamilial relationships in residential rental properties. [28] Additionally, cities cannot impose
conditions on certain property uses that would prevent the continuation of lawful nonconforming
uses. [29] Zoning ordinances are presumed valid, but they must align with state law and cannot
impose standards less stringent than those established by the state. [26] [30]

Application to Data Centers
While cities may regulate the location and operation of data centers through zoning ordinances,
such regulations must not conflict with state laws governing data center businesses. [5] [23] [31]
For instance, a city ordinance imposing additional taxes or fees on data centers could be
preempted if it conflicts with the tax exemptions provided under Iowa Code § 423.3 and 423.4.
[4] [8] [32] Similarly, zoning restrictions that effectively prohibit the establishment of data
centers in areas zoned for industrial or commercial use could be challenged as inconsistent with
state law.
